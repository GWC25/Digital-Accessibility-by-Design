ESOL learners
Learners studying in a language that isn't their first face double the cognitive load. Inaccessible materials — dense PDFs, no captions, no translation support — make every session harder than it needs to be.
🧠
SEND learners
Learners with dyslexia, visual impairments, processing differences, or mental health conditions encounter barriers that accessible design removes entirely. What works for them works better for everyone.
👂
Deaf and hard of hearing learners
A recorded lecture without captions is inaccessible. A Teams meeting without live captions excludes. These aren't edge cases — and the fix takes seconds to enable.
📱
Learners experiencing device or connectivity poverty
Coastal and rural Weston-super-Mare has real connectivity gaps. Learners on low-spec devices or patchy broadband need resources that are lightweight, structured, and simple to navigate.
The FE White Paper assumes all learners have devices, connectivity, and digital confidence. Our context tells a different story. Accessibility isn't an add-on. It's the precondition for learning.

Universal Design for Learning (UDL) research shows that designing for the greatest access needs raises the floor for the entire cohort. Captions help ESOL learners, SEND learners — and anyone revising in a noisy environment.

The philosophy

Accessible by design — not by afterthought

Accessibility is not a feature you add at the end. It is a mindset you bring to the beginning. Here is what that means in practice.

The design principle in one sentence: If you design for the edges — for those with the greatest access needs — you strengthen the experience for everyone in the middle.
The five principles of Accessible by Design
1
Start with access, not content
Before creating any resource, ask: who will use this, and what barriers might they face? Think about a learner with dyslexia, someone without a laptop at home, or a new arrival who is still developing their English. Design for them first — and everyone benefits.
2
Structure is accessibility
Using Heading styles in Word, logical slide order in PowerPoint, and consistent folder structures in Teams makes documents navigable by screen readers and easier to process for learners with cognitive differences. Structure is not bureaucracy — it's care.
3
Never rely on colour alone
Around 8% of men have some form of colour vision deficiency. If your resource uses red for "wrong" and green for "right," it is inaccessible to them. Always pair colour with a shape, label, or pattern. The Accessibility Checker flags contrast issues automatically.
4
Every image needs a text alternative
Alt text describes what an image shows — it's what a screen reader reads aloud for a learner with a visual impairment. Right-click any image → Edit alt text. Two sentences. Thirty seconds. Transforms access entirely.
5
Build it in — don't bolt it on
The goal is for accessibility to become as automatic as saving your file. Word over PDF. Heading styles, always. Accessibility Checker before upload, always. Captions on by default. Always.
